World History — 19th & 20th Century Overview

The Industrial Revolution (c. 1760–1840)
Began in Britain, driven by innovations like the steam engine, spinning jenny, and power loom.
Shifted economies from agrarian/handicraft to machine-based manufacturing in factories.
Urbanization increased rapidly as workers moved to cities for factory jobs.
Led to new social classes: industrial capitalists (bourgeoisie) and factory workers (proletariat).
Spread to continental Europe and the United States through the 19th century, transforming transportation (railroads, steamships) and communication (telegraph).

Nationalism and Unification Movements
Nationalism: the belief that people sharing a common language, culture, and history should form their own nation-state.
Unification of Italy (completed 1871): led by figures like Giuseppe Garibaldi and Camillo di Cavour, unifying separate Italian states.
Unification of Germany (completed 1871): led by Otto von Bismarck through a series of wars, culminating in the proclamation of the German Empire.
Nationalism also fueled independence movements in Latin America and the Balkans.

Imperialism (c. 1870–1914)
European powers, the U.S., and Japan competed to colonize Africa and Asia for raw materials, markets, and strategic advantage.
The "Scramble for Africa" was formalized at the Berlin Conference (1884–85), where European powers divided the continent with little regard for existing ethnic boundaries.
Imperialism was justified by ideas like Social Darwinism and the "civilizing mission."
Resistance movements arose across colonized regions, though most were suppressed by superior military technology.

World War I (1914–1918)
Causes: militarism, alliances (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente), imperialism, and nationalism (often remembered as MAIN).
Triggered by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary in Sarajevo (1914).
Characterized by trench warfare, new technologies (machine guns, poison gas, tanks, airplanes), and massive casualties.
Ended with the Treaty of Versailles (1919), which imposed heavy reparations and territorial losses on Germany.

The Interwar Period and the Rise of Totalitarianism
The Great Depression (starting 1929) caused global economic collapse and high unemployment.
Economic instability contributed to the rise of totalitarian regimes: Fascism in Italy under Mussolini, Nazism in Germany under Hitler, and Stalinism in the Soviet Union.
These regimes featured single-party rule, state control of the economy and media, and suppression of dissent.

World War II (1939–1945)
Began with Germany's invasion of Poland (September 1939).
Major theaters: European theater (Allies vs. Axis powers Germany/Italy) and Pacific theater (Allies vs. Japan).
The Holocaust: the systematic genocide of six million Jews and millions of others by Nazi Germany.
The U.S. entered after the attack on Pearl Harbor (December 1941).
Ended in Europe with Germany's surrender (May 1945) and in the Pacific after atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945).

The Cold War (1947–1991)
A prolonged geopolitical and ideological standoff between the U.S. (capitalism/democracy) and the Soviet Union (communism), without direct large-scale military conflict between the two superpowers.
Key features: the arms race, the Space Race, proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam), and the division of Europe (the "Iron Curtain").
Ended with the fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and the dissolution of the Soviet Union (1991).
