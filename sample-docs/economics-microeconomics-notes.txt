Microeconomics — Core Concepts

Supply and Demand
Demand: the quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices, holding other factors constant.
The Law of Demand: as price increases, quantity demanded decreases (downward-sloping demand curve).
Supply: the quantity of a good producers are willing to sell at various prices.
The Law of Supply: as price increases, quantity supplied increases (upward-sloping supply curve).
Equilibrium: the price and quantity at which supply equals demand. Surpluses occur above equilibrium price; shortages occur below it.
Shifts vs. movements: a change in price causes movement along a curve; a change in any other factor (income, preferences, input costs) shifts the entire curve.

Elasticity
Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price.
Elastic demand (|E| > 1): quantity demanded is very responsive to price changes (e.g., luxury goods).
Inelastic demand (|E| < 1): quantity demanded is not very responsive (e.g., necessities like insulin).
Determinants of elasticity: availability of substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, proportion of income spent, time horizon.

Consumer and Producer Theory
Utility: the satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming a good. Marginal utility is the additional satisfaction from one more unit.
Diminishing marginal utility: each additional unit consumed provides less additional satisfaction than the previous one.
Consumers maximize utility where the marginal utility per dollar is equal across all goods.
Production function: relates inputs (labor, capital) to output. Marginal product is the additional output from one more unit of input.
Diminishing marginal returns: as more of one input is added (holding others fixed), the additional output eventually decreases.

Costs of Production
Fixed costs do not change with output (e.g., rent); variable costs change with output (e.g., materials, labor).
Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost.
Marginal cost: the additional cost of producing one more unit. A firm maximizes profit where marginal revenue = marginal cost.
Average total cost = total cost / quantity. Economies of scale occur when average cost decreases as output increases.

Market Structures
Perfect competition: many small firms, identical products, free entry/exit, firms are "price takers."
Monopoly: a single seller with significant market power and barriers to entry; sets price above marginal cost.
Monopolistic competition: many firms selling differentiated products, some pricing power, free entry/exit.
Oligopoly: a few large firms dominate the market; firms are interdependent and may engage in strategic behavior (e.g., game theory, collusion).

Market Failures and Government Intervention
Externalities: costs or benefits of a transaction that affect third parties (e.g., pollution = negative externality).
Public goods: non-excludable and non-rivalrous (e.g., national defense) — often underprovided by free markets.
Price ceilings (max price, e.g., rent control) can cause shortages if set below equilibrium.
Price floors (min price, e.g., minimum wage) can cause surpluses if set above equilibrium.
Taxes and subsidies: governments use taxes to discourage activities (e.g., taxing pollution) and subsidies to encourage them (e.g., subsidizing renewable energy).

Comparative Advantage and Trade
Absolute advantage: the ability to produce more of a good using the same resources.
Comparative advantage: the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.
Countries (or individuals) gain from trade by specializing in goods where they have a comparative advantage and trading for the rest.
