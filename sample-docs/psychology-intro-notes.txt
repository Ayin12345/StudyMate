Introduction to Psychology — Key Concepts

Major Perspectives in Psychology
Biological: behavior is explained through genetics, brain structures, and neurochemistry.
Behavioral: focuses on observable behavior and how it's shaped by the environment through conditioning.
Cognitive: studies internal mental processes like memory, perception, language, and problem-solving.
Psychodynamic: rooted in Freud's ideas, emphasizes unconscious drives and early childhood experiences.
Humanistic: emphasizes free will, personal growth, and self-actualization (Maslow, Rogers).

Research Methods
Experiments: manipulate an independent variable to observe effects on a dependent variable; allow causal conclusions.
Correlational studies: measure the relationship between two variables without manipulation; cannot establish causation.
Case studies: in-depth examination of a single individual or group; rich detail but limited generalizability.
Surveys: gather self-reported data from large samples; can be affected by response bias.
Confounding variables: outside factors that can affect the outcome and must be controlled for.

Neurons and the Nervous System
Neurons communicate via electrical impulses (action potentials) and chemical signals (neurotransmitters) across synapses.
Key neurotransmitters: dopamine (reward, motivation), serotonin (mood), GABA (inhibition), acetylcholine (muscle action, learning).
The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system (PNS) connects the CNS to the rest of the body.
Major brain structures: the hippocampus (memory formation), amygdala (emotion, fear), prefrontal cortex (decision-making, impulse control), and cerebellum (balance, coordination).

Learning
Classical conditioning (Pavlov): a neutral stimulus becomes associated with an unconditioned stimulus to produce a conditioned response.
Operant conditioning (Skinner): behavior is shaped by reinforcement (increases behavior) and punishment (decreases behavior).
Reinforcement schedules: fixed-ratio, variable-ratio, fixed-interval, variable-interval — variable-ratio schedules produce the highest, most resistant response rates.
Observational learning (Bandura): learning by watching and imitating others (e.g., the Bobo doll experiment).

Memory
Three stages: encoding (input), storage (retention), retrieval (output).
Sensory memory holds information very briefly; short-term/working memory holds a limited amount for a short time (~7 items, ~20-30 seconds).
Long-term memory has a vast capacity and can be divided into explicit (declarative: facts and events) and implicit (procedural: skills and habits) memory.
Forgetting can result from encoding failure, decay, interference, or retrieval failure.

Development
Piaget's stages of cognitive development: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational.
Attachment theory (Bowlby/Ainsworth): early bonds with caregivers shape later relationships; attachment styles include secure, avoidant, and anxious/ambivalent.
Erikson's psychosocial stages describe a series of conflicts (e.g., trust vs. mistrust, identity vs. role confusion) faced across the lifespan.

Social Psychology
Conformity: adjusting behavior to match a group, even without direct pressure (Asch's line experiments).
Obedience: following the orders of an authority figure, even when it conflicts with personal conscience (Milgram's experiments).
Cognitive dissonance: discomfort from holding conflicting beliefs or behaviors, often resolved by changing one's attitude.
Fundamental attribution error: the tendency to overemphasize personality-based explanations for others' behavior while underemphasizing situational factors.
